Introduction to EMR and EHR

What actually separates an EMR from an EHR, how patient data moves between systems, and why it matters for coding accuracy.

EMR vs. EHR: one letter, one real difference

The two terms get used interchangeably, but they describe different scopes of the same idea — a digital patient chart.

TermScopeThink of it as
EMR (Electronic Medical Record)One practice or facilityA digital version of that office's paper chart
EHR (Electronic Health Record)Designed to be shared across providersA more complete, portable patient record

An EMR generally doesn't leave the practice that created it — if you switch doctors, your old EMR data often has to be printed or faxed to the new office. An EHR is built with interoperability in mind, so authorized providers outside the originating practice can pull it up.

An EMR is one provider's digital chart; an EHR is that same information made shareable across the patient's whole care team.

Where this came from: the HITECH Act

The 2009 HITECH Act pushed U.S. healthcare from paper charts to electronic ones, offering incentive payments (originally called "Meaningful Use," later renamed "Promoting Interoperability") to providers who adopted Certified EHR Technology (CEHRT) and used it to meet specific standards for data capture, exchange, and patient access.

Core things an EHR does

FunctionWhat it means
CPOEComputerized Provider Order Entry — orders (labs, meds, imaging) entered directly, not handwritten
e-PrescribingPrescriptions sent electronically straight to the pharmacy
Clinical decision supportBuilt-in alerts — drug interactions, allergy warnings, preventive care reminders
Patient portalPatients view results, message providers, and request records themselves
Structured documentationTemplates, problem lists, and coded fields (vs. free-text notes) that make data reportable

Common EHR platforms you'll hear about

You don't need to be an expert in any specific system to start a coding or billing career, but recognizing the names helps in interviews and job postings: Epic, Oracle Health (formerly Cerner), MEDITECH, athenahealth, and eClinicalWorks are among the most widely used in U.S. hospitals and clinics.

Interoperability: HL7 and FHIR

For an EHR to actually share data with other systems, everyone needs to speak the same technical language. HL7 (Health Level Seven) is the longstanding messaging standard for exchanging clinical data; FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) is the newer, web-based standard built for modern apps and patient-facing tools, and is now the standard behind most new health data exchange requirements.

Why this matters for coders and billers

  • Superbills and charge capture often generate automatically from what's documented in the EHR — if the documentation is incomplete, the charges are wrong before a coder even sees the chart.
  • Computer-Assisted Coding (CAC) tools suggest codes based on EHR documentation using natural language processing — coders review and correct the suggestions rather than starting from a blank page.
  • Documentation integrity is a real risk area: copy-forward notes, templated "normal" exams that don't match what actually happened, and auto-populated fields can all create records that don't support the codes billed.

Note: "cloning" (copying a previous note forward without updating it to reflect today's visit) is one of the most commonly cited documentation risks in payer audits — it's worth knowing the term even before your first coding job.

The bottom line

You'll spend your career reading EHR-generated documentation, not paper charts, so understanding what the system captures well (and where it can quietly go wrong) is just as important as knowing the code sets themselves.
