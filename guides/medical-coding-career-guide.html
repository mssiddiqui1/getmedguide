Medical Coding Career Guide

A complete walkthrough of how to break into medical billing and coding — which certification to chase, a step-by-step plan to land your first job, and how to actually pass the CPC exam.

Before you pay for anything: exam fees, code sets, and certification rules change every year. Always confirm current details on the AAPC, AHIMA, and NHA websites before enrolling in a program or scheduling an exam.

1. Which certification exam should you take?

Most training programs let you sit for one of three entry-level certifications: the CPC, the CCA, or the CBCS. Here's an honest comparison of the three:

CPC (AAPC)CCA (AHIMA)CBCS (NHA)
RecognitionHighest — the industry gold standardModerateEntry-level / billing-focused
Best forPhysician offices, clinics, outpatient, payers, remote codingBroad (hospital + outpatient)Billing & insurance front-end roles
Typical exam cost~$399–$499~$199–$299~$117
DifficultyHardest (~50–70% first-time pass rate)MediumEasiest
Typical payHighestMiddleLowest

The general recommendation is the CPC — it's the credential most requested in U.S. job postings for outpatient and remote coding roles, and it opens the most doors. If a training program includes a voucher, it's usually worth putting it toward the CPC rather than a cheaper exam, since the voucher absorbs the higher cost either way.

One smart hedge: many programs prepare you for more than one exam. If you're consistently scoring 75%+ on practice exams, aim for the CPC. If test anxiety is a real concern, the CBCS is a lower-stakes fallback — but if your program includes two CPC attempts, there's little reason not to aim straight for it.

2. The full plan to get a job

Think of it as five phases over roughly 6–10 months.

Phase 1 — Finish your training & learn the manuals (Months 1–3)

  • Work through every module of your program, especially ICD-10-CM, CPT, HCPCS Level II, ICD-10-PCS, medical terminology, and anatomy.
  • Get comfortable flipping through your current-year code books quickly — you'll use them in the exam, so speed matters as much as knowledge.
  • Learn a real encoder tool (many programs include one, like Codify) — it's both an exam-prep aid and a strong interview talking point.
  • If your program includes an AAPC student membership, use it — it unlocks practice exams, forums, and your local chapter network.

Phase 2 — Pass the CPC exam (Months 3–5)

Follow the exam study plan in Section 5 below, then sit for the exam — either online proctored from home or at a test center. Most people pass with a CPC-A designation (the "A" stands for Apprentice, since you don't have documented work experience yet). That's completely normal, and you can already apply for jobs with a CPC-A.

Phase 3 — Remove the "Apprentice" status without a job (Months 4–6, can overlap)

This is the part most people miss. Removing the "A" requires the equivalent of 2 years of experience credit, and AAPC lets you earn both years without being employed yet:

  • Year 1 — a coding practicum. Programs like AAPC's Practicode put you through hundreds of real, redacted coding cases. Finish with a strong score and AAPC applies a full year of experience credit automatically.
  • Year 2 — an education waiver. A sufficiently long coding course (AAPC generally looks for 80+ contact hours, documented on a certificate or transcript) can satisfy the second year. Confirm directly with AAPC member services whether your specific program and its certificate qualify before you rely on it — requirements and acceptable documentation can change.

Sequence matters: AAPC does not credit education or practicum work completed before you pass the exam. The order has to be: pass the CPC → complete the practicum and keep your training certificate → file for apprentice removal.

Phase 4 — Build a resume that gets callbacks (Months 5–7)

  • Lead with your credential (CPC or CPC-A) and the code sets you're trained in.
  • List any coding practicum or externship — it reads like real experience to a recruiter.
  • Call out hands-on encoder experience and familiarity with claim forms (CMS-1500 / UB-04).
  • Consider a second credential like the CPB (Certified Professional Biller) — being both a biller and coder opens more roles.

Phase 5 — Apply strategically (Months 6–10)

Medical coding is heavily remote-friendly, so cast a wide net: search terms like "remote CPC-A," "junior coder," and "coding trainee." Also look locally at hospital systems, physician groups, and billing companies. Stepping-stone titles that hire entry-level candidates and lead toward coding include medical records/HIM clerk, billing specialist, claims processor, charge entry, denials/AR specialist, and patient access. Staffing agencies that specialize in health information management and revenue cycle roles are another good route, and your local AAPC chapter is one of the best places to network into a first job.

Apply in volume, tailor your resume's keywords to each posting, and don't be discouraged by "experience required" listings — a practicum plus certification often satisfies it in practice.

3. Can you start working before certification?

Yes. Several roles hire before you're certified and let you earn while you study — and the experience often counts toward removing a CPC-A "A" later:

  • Medical billing specialist / billing clerk
  • Claims processor / charge entry
  • Medical records / HIM clerk
  • Patient access, registration, or insurance verification
  • Accounts receivable (AR) / denials specialist
  • Scheduler / prior-authorization assistant

Any of these gets you inside a healthcare revenue-cycle department, earning income and building real, relevant experience while you study.

4. After you're certified — where the ladder goes

Once you hold a CPC and have a year or two of experience, the career opens up quickly:

  • Specialty coding (higher pay): CPB (billing), COC (outpatient facility), CIC (inpatient facility), CRC (risk adjustment — very in-demand and remote-friendly), CCS (hospital inpatient gold standard).
  • Auditing & compliance (senior, higher pay): CPMA (auditor), CDEO/CDI (clinical documentation improvement), CPCO (compliance officer).
  • Management & analytics: coding team lead → coding manager → HIM director; revenue cycle analyst; denials management lead.

Industry salary surveys consistently show certified coders out-earning non-certified staff, with the gap widening further for coders who hold multiple credentials or move into specialty and auditing roles. A realistic multi-year arc looks like: CPC-A → CPC → add a specialty credential (like CPB or CRC) → build experience → move into auditing or a senior/remote coding role.

5. Full guide to passing the CPC exam

The exam at a glance

  • 100 multiple-choice questions, including roughly 10 case-study (operative-note) questions.
  • 4 hours, one sitting — about 2 minutes 24 seconds per question, so pacing is the #1 challenge.
  • Open book — you bring your own printed CPT, ICD-10-CM, and HCPCS Level II manuals (online-proctored exams typically require physical books, not digital).
  • 70% is the passing score. Results typically arrive in about 7–10 business days.
  • Delivered online (proctored from home) or at a test center.

First-time pass rates run roughly 50–70% — open book does not mean easy. It rewards knowing exactly where to look, fast.

What's tested (rough weighting)

  • CPT procedure coding is the single biggest block — roughly half the exam — spanning surgery by body system, E/M, anesthesia, radiology, pathology/lab, and medicine.
  • E/M (evaluation & management) alone is roughly 15–20% — know the current office-visit leveling rules cold.
  • ICD-10-CM diagnosis coding across body systems.
  • HCPCS Level II, modifiers, anatomy/physiology/medical terminology, and compliance/regulatory topics (HIPAA, NCCI edits).

A 10-week study plan (about 12–15 hours/week)

WeeksFocus
1–2Medical terminology + anatomy by body system. Start tabbing your code books.
3–4ICD-10-CM guidelines and practice (sequencing, "code first," combination codes, laterality).
5–7CPT by section, with extra time on Surgery and E/M — the heaviest material.
8HCPCS Level II and modifiers (25, 59, 26/TC, 50, 51, 76/77, RT/LT, 57, 24).
9Two full, timed, open-book practice exams. Review every wrong answer.
10Targeted review of weak areas, one more timed exam, then test day.

The open-book strategy — this is what separates a pass from a fail

  • Tab your manuals. Color-coded tabs for each CPT section, ICD-10-CM chapter, and HCPCS letter. Highlighting is fine; long handwritten notes usually are not allowed — check your exam's current rules.
  • Practice looking up, not memorizing. You can look up any code during the exam — lookup speed is the actual skill being tested. Run timed lookup drills.
  • Use a 3-pass method: answer everything you can quickly first (never more than ~4 minutes on one question — flag and move on), return to flagged questions for the longer lookups, then finish the case studies and anything still open. Never leave a question blank — there's no penalty for guessing.
  • Eliminate wrong answers first. Most coding questions have two plausible distractors — rule those out, then verify the remaining option in the book.
  • Read E/M and operative-note cases twice — the detail that determines the correct code is easy to miss on a first read.
  • Take at least two full timed practice exams before test day. Official practice exams from your certifying body are the closest thing to the real test.

Practice questions

A sample across the topics the CPC exam covers. Codes and ranges change yearly — treat any specific numbers below as illustrative, and always confirm against your current-year manuals.

The suffix "-ectomy" means:

Surgical removal. ("-otomy" is incision into; "-ostomy" is creation of a new opening; "-scopy" is visual examination.)

Which chamber of the heart pumps oxygenated blood to the body?

The left ventricle.

A patient has type 2 diabetes with diabetic neuropathy. How should this be coded in ICD-10-CM?

Use a combination code that links the diabetes and the manifestation together, rather than coding each condition separately.

What does the instruction "code first" mean under an ICD-10-CM code?

It means you should sequence the underlying/etiology code before this one — not skip either code.

A CPT code marked with a "+" symbol is a(n):

Add-on code — reported together with a primary procedure code, never billed alone.

HCPCS Level II codes are mainly used for:

Drugs, supplies, durable medical equipment (DME), and services not found in CPT.

Modifier 25 is appended to indicate:

A significant, separately identifiable E/M service performed on the same day as a procedure.

What does "upcoding" mean, and why does it matter?

Billing a higher level of service than what was actually documented — it's considered fraud and carries serious compliance risk.

The CMS-1500 form is used to bill:

Physician / professional (outpatient) claims. The UB-04 is used for institutional/hospital facility claims instead.

A patient comes in for a scheduled minor procedure. During the same visit, the physician also evaluates and treats a new, unrelated complaint, fully documented as a separate E/M service. What modifier goes on the E/M code so both get paid?

Modifier 25 — a significant, separately identifiable E/M service on the same day as a procedure.
