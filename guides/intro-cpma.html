Introduction to CPMA

What medical auditing involves, the audit cycle, and what the CPMA credential tests beyond coding itself.

What CPMA is

The Certified Professional Medical Auditor (CPMA), from AAPC, is not a coding credential in the usual sense — it certifies the ability to audit coding and documentation for accuracy and compliance, rather than to assign codes from scratch. Auditors check whether the codes billed actually match what the documentation supports.

The audit cycle

The audit cycle is a loop — re-audit closes back to selecting the next sample once corrective action is in place.

What auditors actually evaluate

  • Whether documentation supports the level of E/M service billed
  • Whether diagnosis codes reflect what's actually documented (not just what the payer prefers)
  • Whether modifiers were used correctly and are supported by the note
  • Medical necessity — not just "was it coded right," but "should it have been billed at all"

Key knowledge areas

AreaWhy it matters
E/M guidelines (1995/1997 vs. 2021+)Auditors need to apply the correct framework depending on the date of service and service type
Statistical samplingAuditors typically review a representative sample, not every chart — sample size and method affect how defensible the findings are
Risk areas / OIG Work PlanFederal audit priorities (e.g., high-level E/M visits, certain modifiers) that internal auditors track proactively
Compliance plansHow findings translate into corrective action, provider education, and policy updates

Exam format

DetailWhat to expect
Administered byAAPC
Prerequisite knowledgeSolid coding foundation is expected — CPMA builds on coding knowledge rather than replacing it
FocusAudit methodology, E/M leveling, risk analysis, compliance

Who should pursue CPMA

Experienced coders moving into compliance, quality, or auditing roles — often a natural next step after a few years of hands-on coding, since auditing requires already knowing the coding rules well enough to know when they've been broken.

Note: auditing and coding are complementary but different skill sets — a great coder isn't automatically a great auditor. Auditing adds sampling methodology, report-writing, and the ability to explain why a finding is a finding to a provider who may disagree.
